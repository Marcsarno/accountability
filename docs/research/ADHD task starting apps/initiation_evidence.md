# Task initiation, procrastination, time perception and prospective memory in adult ADHD: evidence and interventions

Strength scale used throughout (for the report writer):
- **Strong**: several RCTs or meta-analyses in adults with ADHD that agree.
- **Moderate**: one or two adult RCTs, or a meta-analysis that mixes ages or populations.
- **Weak / indirect**: cross-sectional or correlational studies, child-only evidence, general-population evidence carried over to ADHD, small pilots, or expert opinion.
- **None found**: no controlled evidence found in this search.

Method note: about 25 searches and fetches (PubMed/PMC, publisher pages, trial registries, secondary summaries). Full texts were read for Matsumoto et al. 2024 (component network meta-analysis), Fuermaier et al. 2013 (prospective memory) and Schofield et al. 2026 (scoping review). Other figures come from abstracts or secondary summaries, as marked.

## 1. CBT for adult ADHD (Safren; Solanto): which components target organisation, planning, procrastination and initiation, and were they ever isolated?

### Takeaway
Skills-based CBT for adult ADHD works. Two landmark RCTs (n=86 and n=88) and meta-analyses show small-to-medium effects over active controls (**Strong** for CBT as a package). Its core is organisation and planning: a calendar plus a single task list, prioritisation, breaking tasks down, and anti-procrastination skills. No adult trial has isolated a single component. The best component-level evidence is a 2024 component network meta-analysis (adolescents and adults pooled). It found that "organisational strategies" (organisation, planning, time management) roughly doubled the odds of treatment response (**Moderate**, indirect at component level).

### Cited Findings
- **Safren et al. 2010, JAMA 304(8):875-880.** RCT, 86 adults with ADHD on medication who still had symptoms. They received 12 individual sessions of CBT ("Mastering Your Adult ADHD") or relaxation with educational support (an attention-matched control). Assessors were blind to condition. CBT gave lower post-treatment scores on clinician-rated CGI and the ADHD rating scale. Responder rates were 53% vs 23% (CGI) and 67% vs 33% (ADHD-RS). CBT responders kept their gains at 6 and 12 months. 79 of 86 completed treatment and 70 completed follow-up — [Abstract reprint (anhoriga.se)](https://anhoriga.se/Artiklar-och-rapporter/2010/cognitive-behavioral-therapy-vs-relaxation-with-educational-support-for-medication-treated-adults-with-adhd-and-persistent-symptoms-a-randomized-controlled-trial); [Global Family Doctor summary](https://www.globalfamilydoctor.com/Resources/A-Ztopiclisting/JournalWatch/CognitivebehavioraltherapyfortreatmentofadultADHD.aspx). One trade-press article says 43 were randomised. That conflicts with the abstract's 86 and is likely an error — [MDedge PDF](https://cdn.mdedge.com/files/s3fs-public/issues/articles/70329_main_10.pdf).
- **Content of the Safren programme.** It is modular. Module 1 is psychoeducation plus organisation and planning, over about 4 sessions, and teaches a calendar and task-list system for appointments, schedule and to-dos. Later modules cover distractibility and adaptive thinking; the trial literature also describes a procrastination module. The publisher calls it the only individual psychosocial treatment for adult ADHD with RCT support, but that is the authors' own claim — [OUP, Therapist Guide 2nd edn](https://academic.oup.com/book/1240). The full module list was not verified from primary text, so it is also listed under Gaps.
- **Solanto et al. 2010, Am J Psychiatry.** RCT, 88 adults aged 18-65, single site in the USA. Participants had 12 weekly 2-hour group sessions of meta-cognitive therapy (MCT) targeting time management, organisation and planning, or group supportive psychotherapy matched for therapist time and attention. Both arms were on medication. MCT gave significantly larger improvements in inattention severity. Responders (at least 30% drop in inattention) were 53% vs 28% — [Am J Psychiatry abstract](https://psychiatryonline.org/doi/abs/10.1176/appi.ajp.2009.09081123); [Cochrane review table](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6494390/table/CD010840-tblf-0012); [ScienceDaily press release](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2010/03/100330142437.htm). Later reviews describe the trial as small and single-site (same sources).
- **Knouse et al. 2017 meta-analysis.** 32 studies, up to 896 participants. Against controls, CBT had g = 0.65 for symptoms and g = 0.51 for functioning. Studies with active control groups showed smaller effects — [PubMed 28504540](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28504540/).
- **Earlier pooled estimate (Young et al. 2016), as tabulated in López-Pinar et al. 2018.** CBT vs waitlist SMD = 0.76 (95% CI 0.21-1.31). CBT vs active control SMD = 0.43 (95% CI 0.14-0.71). For comparison, stimulants have SMD of about 0.72 on symptoms — [López-Pinar et al. 2018, Front Psychol](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5946687).
- **Matsumoto, Hamatani, Kunisato & Mizuno 2024, BMJ Mental Health 27:e301303.** Component network meta-analysis of 43 RCTs, 3,817 participants: 56.8% adults (n=2,169) and 43.2% adolescents (n=1,648), ages 10-60. "Organisational strategies" (organisation, planning, time management, applied behaviour analysis techniques) gave incremental OR 2.03 (95% CI 1.27-3.24) for treatment response. Third-wave components (mindfulness, ACT) gave iOR 1.95 (1.30-2.93). Problem-solving gave iSMD 0.42 (0.01-0.83) on inattention, per the abstract; the results section reports 0.98, which is internally inconsistent. No component was linked to dropout. Reminders and homework were not analysed as components. Risk of bias was low in 32.6% of trials, "some concerns" in 55.8% and high in 11.6%. The authors call the findings hypotheses that need confirmatory trials — [PMC11683884](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11683884/). The protocol defines organisational components to include organisation, planning and time management — [BMJ Open protocol](https://bmjopen.bmj.com/content/13/4/e068547).
- **NICE.** Secondary summaries of NICE NG87 (2018) say it recommends considering non-drug treatment for adults who choose not to take medication or have trouble adhering, and combined treatment when symptoms persist on medication. The minimum is "a structured supportive psychological intervention focused on ADHD" plus regular follow-up, and it may include elements of CBT or a full course. An NIHR review summary found CBT beat supportive therapy on clinician-rated symptoms but not on self-reported symptoms, with low-quality evidence in both cases — [NIHR Evidence alert](https://evidence.nihr.ac.uk/alert/cognitive-behavioural-therapy-could-benefit-adults-with-attention-deficit-hyperactivity-disorder-adhd/). This is not verified against the NG87 text itself.

### Inferences
- The well-supported "active ingredients" a planner can carry over are: one trusted capture list plus a calendar that is checked daily, prioritising the list, breaking large tasks into small first steps, and scheduling tasks into specific times. These come from organisational strategies and MCT. They are supported at package level (**Strong**) and as a component class (**Moderate**). No specific app mechanic has been isolated.
- Both RCTs ran 12 weekly sessions with a therapist. The weekly human review and accountability loop is part of what was tested. A self-guided app does not reproduce it automatically (see section 7 on adherence).
- Both landmark trials enrolled medicated adults. Generalising to unmedicated users is less certain; Safren's authors say so explicitly.

### Gaps
- No adult dismantling trial isolates the organisation/planning module, the procrastination module, or the task-initiation techniques themselves.
- Solanto 2010's specific MCT techniques were not verified from primary text in this search. Examples are the "if it is not in the planner, it does not exist" rule, the "getting started is the hardest part" cognitive restructuring, and making the first step small. Treat them as unverified.
- Precise Safren module names and session counts beyond module 1 were not verified from primary text.
- NICE NG87 and CADDRA guideline wording was not read directly. Verify at nice.org.uk/guidance/ng87 and caddra.ca.

## 2. Implementation intentions (if-then plans) in ADHD

### Takeaway
In general populations, if-then plans have a medium-to-large effect on goal attainment: d = 0.65 across 94 tests (**Strong** for general populations). In ADHD, the evidence is lab studies of **children** showing better inhibition, task-shifting and delay of gratification (**Weak/indirect** for adults). No adult ADHD trial of implementation intentions as a standalone intervention was found.

### Cited Findings
- **Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006,** Advances in Experimental Social Psychology 38:69-119. Meta-analysis of 94 independent tests from 63 reports, more than 8,000 participants. If-then plans had a medium-to-large effect on goal attainment, d = 0.65. Most comparisons were against people who had formed goal intentions only — [KOPS Konstanz record](https://kops.uni-konstanz.de/entities/publication/2e749bfb-8533-437c-8203-7e788c910c5f).
- **Gawrilow, Gollwitzer & Oettingen 2011 (JSCP), children.** Children with ADHD made fewer perseverative errors on a set-shifting task when they had formed if-then plans — [PDF](https://www.socmot.uni-konstanz.de/sites/default/files/Gawrilow_Gollwitzer_Oettingen_Executive_Function_JSCP_2011.pdf).
- **Gawrilow & Gollwitzer 2008, children.** With if-then plans, children with ADHD improved Go/No-Go response inhibition to the level of children without ADHD — [PDF](https://www.socmot.uni-konstanz.de/sites/default/files/08_Gawrilow_Gollwitzer_Implementation_intentions_facilit.pdf).
- **Gawrilow, Gollwitzer & Oettingen 2011, delay study, children.** 45 inpatient children with ADHD increased delay of gratification after forming implementation intentions — [PDF](https://www.socmot.uni-konstanz.de/sites/default/files/Gawrilow_Gollwitzer_Oettingen_Delay_2011-2.pdf).
- **APSARD clinical-practice note (2018).** If-then plans are used inside CBT for adult ADHD, mainly for procrastination. Implementation intentions "had not yet been studied in adults with ADHD." The only adult data cited was a small open pilot of 5 adults receiving CBT without medication — [APSARD](https://apsard.org/?p=4314).
- **Mental-health populations.** A meta-analysis summarised by the Mental Elf concluded that people with mental health diagnoses benefit when prompted to form if-then plans (when, where, how) — [National Elf Service](https://nationalelfservice.net/social-care/support-planning/implementation-intentions-helping-people-achieve-their-goals).

### Inferences
- Asking the user to attach a specific cue to each task ("When I sit down after breakfast, I will open the bill email") has strong general-population support and plausible ADHD support. It is cheap to build: a "when/where" field or prompt at task creation.
- Event-based (situational) if-then cues fit the prospective-memory findings in section 4. Execution and cue response were relatively intact there, while planning was the weak point. So the app should do the planning scaffolding and let the cue trigger the action.

### Gaps
- No RCT of implementation intentions in adults with ADHD was found. Adult PubMed searches may have missed post-2018 studies.
- A 2024 meta-analysis by Sheeran, Listrom & Gollwitzer (642 tests, d about 0.27-0.66 by outcome) appeared only in an unattributed secondary snippet. Not verified.

## 3. Time perception and "time blindness"; do external timers or visual time aids help?

### Takeaway
Meta-analytic evidence (all ages pooled) shows broad timing deficits in ADHD, in time estimation, production and reproduction (**Moderate**). The adult-specific literature is sparse and mixed (**Weak**). No controlled evidence was found that external or visual timers improve outcomes in adults with ADHD (**None found**). The one peer-reviewed visual-timer study was in typical 7-9-year-olds and measured anxiety.

### Cited Findings
- **Marx et al. 2021, JAACAP.** Meta-analysis of 55 studies (searched to Sept 2020), random effects, all ages. It found deficits in time estimation and production accuracy, consistent with an "accelerated internal clock". It found additional deficits in time reproduction, involving attentional and motivational factors, and concludes there is "a broad range of timing deficits" in ADHD — [em-consulte abstract](https://www.em-consulte.com/article/1527936/meta-analysis-altered-perceptual-timing-abilities-); [Southampton eprint](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/456003). Adult subgroup results were not checked.
- **Mette 2023, IJERPH.** Narrative review of the past decade of adult studies. Research on adult time perception is "very scarce" and the results are mixed: some studies show deficits in estimation and reproduction, others do not. The author attributes this to differences in method — [PMC9962130](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9962130/).
- **Pironti et al. 2016, Psychological Medicine.** Adults with ADHD were impaired in temporal reproduction — [Cambridge](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/journals/psychological-medicine/article/temporal-reproduction-and-its-neuroanatomical-correlates-in-adults-with-attention-deficit-hyperactivity-disorder-and-their-unaffected-firstdegree-relatives/9AF7D4EDAA163A77450E2879537ED711).
- **Kolsuz Kaya & Kaya 2024,** adult case-control, conference-level source. Time-perception impairment was associated with procrastination, especially at longer intervals — [PDF](https://www.turkpsikiyatri.com/Yayin/uploads/bildiriler/3/5.pdf).
- **Visual timer study (non-ADHD children aged 7-9).** A Time Timer lowered anticipatory anxiety before a timed maths task. ADHD status was not the focus — [PMC12731990](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12731990/).
- **Manufacturer and blog claims** of efficacy in ADHD were found, for example a "68% increase in completed tasks" credited to a 2021 Journal of Attention Disorders study with no citation. None could be traced to primary studies; treat them as unsupported — [Time Timer page](https://timetimer.com/pages/time-timer-in-special-needs).

### Inferences
- Making time visible is plausible and low-risk: a remaining-time bar, "starts in 20 min", an elapsed-time display. Its rationale rests on the deficit evidence, not on intervention evidence. Label it as reasonable design, not an evidence-based treatment.
- Time estimation errors probably feed into planning failures and procrastination. Scaffolds like "how long will this take?" plus a later comparison with actual time are plausible. They are untested in ADHD.

### Gaps
- No RCT of timers or visual time aids in adults with ADHD was found.
- No adult-only meta-analysis of time perception in ADHD was found.

## 4. Prospective memory (remembering to do things later) in adult ADHD; external reminders as compensatory aids

### Takeaway
Adults with ADHD show prospective-memory problems. In the best-characterised study, the deficit lay mainly in **forming a good plan** (d = 1.60). Recalling the plan, starting on cue and carrying it out were much less impaired (**Moderate** evidence for the deficit, small samples). External reminders are near-universal clinical advice and part of CBT packages. However, no RCT isolating reminders or alarms for everyday prospective memory in adults with ADHD was found (**Weak/indirect**). The one adult ADHD RCT of SMS reminders (for engagement with an online programme) found they sped up logins but did not improve completion (**Moderate**, see section 7).

### Cited Findings
- **Fuermaier et al. 2013, PLoS ONE.** 45 unmedicated adult outpatients with ADHD vs 45 controls matched on age, sex and IQ, on a complex multi-step prospective-memory task. Results by component:
  - task planning d = 1.60, large (11.6 vs 20.3, p<.001)
  - task switching d = 0.94
  - plan fidelity in execution d = 0.43, not significant after correction
  - plan recall d = 0.03
  - self-initiation on an event cue: 18/45 vs 25/45 succeeded, not significant, ω = 0.16

  The authors conclude: "Impairments of prospective memory mainly emerged from deficient planning abilities." They advise that intentions be "carefully and elaborately planned", with external help from a therapist, coach or family member. Inhibition (Stroop) explained 13.3% of planning variance. Limitations: small sample, a single event cue, a short one-off task — [PMC3590133](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3590133/).
- **Small event-based prospective-memory study, 18 vs 18 adults.** Adults with ADHD performed worse than controls under both inhibitory-load conditions — [MetaTOC record](https://metatoc.com/papers/28935-do-inhibitory-control-demands-affect-event-based-prospective-memory-performance-in-adhd).
- **Related deficit.** Meta-analysis of 38 studies: moderate working-memory deficits in adults with ADHD — [PubMed 23688211](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23688211/).
- **Clinical guidance (CHADD, 2025, expert opinion).** Recommends event-based cues (tying a task to an existing routine) over clock-based cues, layering multiple cues (a phone reminder plus a physical note), and replacing "I'll remember" with external reminders — [CHADD Attention magazine](https://chadd.org/attention-article/remembering-the-future-how-adhd-affects-prospective-memory-and-how-to-work-with-it/).
- **Non-ADHD evidence.** In older adults, reminders improved prospective-memory performance, apparently by reducing monitoring demands — [PMC 11781985](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/11781985/). Seen via a search snippet only; full text not read.

### Inferences
- For "remembering to check the planning app": a passive app is itself a prospective-memory task, which is exactly the kind of task that fails. The app should therefore send an external cue (a notification) and attach checking to an existing routine (an event cue, for example "after coffee").
- Plan recall and cue response were relatively spared, while planning was impaired. So the highest-value help is likely at planning time: specific when/where, concrete first step, realistic sequencing. More alarms help less.
- Expect alert fatigue. Surman et al., cited in Schofield 2026, found only 22% of participants answered every SMS prompt (section 7).

### Gaps
- No systematic review or meta-analysis of prospective memory in adult ADHD was found.
- No RCT of phone reminders or alarms as compensatory aids for daily prospective memory in adults with ADHD was found.

## 5. Procrastination and ADHD: emotional regulation, task aversiveness, delay aversion, reward sensitivity; what helps

### Takeaway
Procrastination is consistently associated with ADHD symptoms, especially inattention. In cross-sectional adult and college samples, emotion dysregulation and low self-esteem partly account for the link (**Weak to moderate**, correlational). Meta-analyses show steeper delay discounting and a preference for small immediate rewards in ADHD (**Moderate**, mixed ages). This supports making rewards and progress immediate. No ADHD-specific trial testing task breakdown or immediate rewards for procrastination in isolation was found. These techniques reach users only as parts of CBT and MCT packages.

### Cited Findings
- **Bodalski et al. 2022/23, college students.** Difficulties in emotion regulation and self-esteem partly accounted for the link between ADHD symptoms and procrastination. Cross-sectional; the authors call for longitudinal work — [BSL record](https://mijn.bsl.nl/adhd-symptoms-and-procrastination-in-college-students-the-roles-/23602698).
- **Netzer Turgeman & Pollak, online adult survey (n=640).** Inattention symptoms related to all 7 motivational and goal-orientation variables tested, and 3 of them directly contributed to procrastination. The authors recommend targeting these mediators in interventions — [HUJI CRIS](https://cris.huji.ac.il/en/publications/what-are-you-waiting-for-roles-of-motivation-goal-orientation-and/).
- **Adult cross-sectional study (n=132).** Procrastination fully mediated the link between ADHD and lower quality of life across four domains. Reported via a secondary summary — [Simply Psychology](https://www.simplypsychology.org/?p=95499).
- **Jackson & MacKillop 2016 meta-analysis.** 25 case-control comparisons, N=3,913. Delay discounting was significantly elevated in ADHD vs controls (mixed ages) — [PubMed 27722208](https://www.pubmed.gov/27722208/).
- **Delay aversion theory (Sonuga-Barke).** Separates steeper discounting from acquired delay aversion, a negative emotional reaction to imposed delay. The adult Quick Delay Questionnaire measures both. A 2024 validation found combined-type ADHD scored higher on delay aversion than inattentive type — [BMC Psychiatry 2024](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12888-024-05706-2).
- **Problem-solving** (breaking problems down and weighing specific solutions) gave iSMD 0.42 on inattention in the component network meta-analysis — [PMC11683884](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11683884/).

### Inferences
- Design levers consistent with the evidence:
  - Shrink the first step so starting feels less aversive.
  - Make reward or feedback immediate: instant visible progress on a tap, not weekly summaries.
  - Avoid shaming language, since low self-esteem and emotion dysregulation mediate procrastination (see section 8).
- None of these levers is supported as a standalone ADHD intervention. Present them as theory-consistent components of evidence-based packages.

### Gaps
- No RCT isolating task breakdown, immediate rewards or gamification for procrastination in adult ADHD was found.
- A Marx et al. 2021 meta-analysis of delay aversion and discounting paradigms (37 comparisons, about 3,763 participants; real rewards roughly doubled the effect) appeared only in a search summary without a verified link.
- No adult longitudinal or causal evidence that emotion dysregulation drives procrastination in ADHD was found.

## 6. Body doubling / social facilitation

### Takeaway
No peer-reviewed controlled trial of body doubling in adults with ADHD was found (**None found / Weak**). The evidence is self-report surveys by providers, small human-computer-interaction prototypes, and one null student poster. Users commonly cite accountability as the reason it helps.

### Cited Findings
- **2025 VR design paper (arXiv).** States that no peer-reviewed controlled trials have examined body doubling in adults with ADHD. Its own study had 12 participants with ADHD comparing alone, human and AI body doubles in VR. Results were not extracted — [arXiv 2509.12153](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.12153v1).
- **Drake University poster (2025).** A brain-computer-interface measure found no significant effect of body doubling on performance, focus or calm. The authors suggest the design may miss its role in starting tasks — [CCSC 2025 poster](https://www.ccsc.org/centralplains/2025/talks/talk-poster16/).
- **UK survey of 117 adults with ADHD.** Self-reported faster re-engagement and steadier focus with virtual body doubling. Run by the organisation that provides the sessions, so there is a conflict of interest — [Neurodiversity.org](https://neurodiversity.org/?p=11449).

### Inferences
- A shared or "co-working" mode is low-risk and popular but unproven. If included, label it as an experiment, not an evidence-based feature. A cheaper evidence-adjacent proxy is accountability: telling someone the plan. That proxy is also unproven in ADHD specifically.

### Gaps
- No RCT, and no peer-reviewed social-facilitation study, specific to adult ADHD.

## 7. Digital interventions for adult ADHD (2020-2026): apps, reminders, self-monitoring

### Takeaway
The adult digital-ADHD literature is large but low quality. A 2026 scoping review found 133 studies, of which 63 were treatment studies. Common problems were lack of blinding, no power analyses, and poor or unreported adherence (**Weak** overall). The one adult RCT that tested reminders directly (SMS nudges in a self-guided programme, n=109) found faster logins but **no** improvement in module completion or practice of coping strategies (**Moderate**, null for core adherence). Self-monitoring by SMS also had poor engagement. Disengagement is the central design problem.

### Cited Findings
- **Schofield et al. 2026, Front Digit Health 8:1746732.** Scoping review of digital health technologies for adults with ADHD, searched to December 2025, 133 studies.
  - **Treatment studies (63):** mostly web/app CBT or psychoeducation (26), cognitive training (13), transcranial stimulation (12) and neurofeedback (9).
  - **Risk of bias in controlled studies:** 65.79% had no participant or provider blinding, 63.15% had no blinded assessors, and 63.15% had no power analysis. 26.32% reported low adherence and 34.21% did not report adherence.
  - **Pre-post studies:** 58.82% lost more than 20% of participants to follow-up.
  - **Engagement examples:** one cognitive-training study (Marcelle et al.) was abandoned at 38% completion. In a symptom-monitoring study (Surman et al.), only 70 of 206 completed exit measures and only 22% replied to all SMS prompts. The authors stress balancing "the burden of continuous prompting with the value that users receive."
  - **Recommendation:** participatory design with people with ADHD, as with "myADHD", which was redesigned after early adherence problems.
  - [Front Digit Health PDF](https://public-pages-files-2025.frontiersin.org/journals/digital-health/articles/10.3389/fdgth.2026.1746732/pdf).
- **Nordby et al. 2022, Front Digit Health 4:821031.** Micro-randomised trial in 109 adults with self-reported ADHD in the self-guided "myADHD" programme. Those who had not finished the weekly module within 2 days were randomised to an SMS reminder or none. Reminders raised the chance of logging in within 48 h in module 2 only, sped logins in modules 3 and 5, and increased time spent on some modules. They had **no effect** on module completion, number of logins or practice of coping strategies. NCT04511169. Background cited: 77% non-completion in an earlier adult ADHD internet intervention — [PMC9149073](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9149073/).
- **Kenter et al. 2023, Internet Interventions 32:100614.** RCT of the self-guided internet intervention for adults with ADHD. Cited by Schofield et al.; results not read in this search — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.invent.2023.100614).
- **Hosseinnia et al. 2025, Front Public Health.** PRISMA systematic review (search to May 2024) of 14 studies of ADHD apps, children and adults. Mixed results: for example, the FOCUS ADHD app was well accepted but did not improve treatment compliance. The authors call for long-term validation — [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/public-health/articles/10.3389/fpubh.2025.1483923/full).
- **Alghory 2025.** Systematic review of digital CBT apps for ADHD, children and adults. Search limited to PubMed and Google Scholar, so methods are weaker — [PMC12436941](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12436941/).
- **Strathclyde/DHI report (2024).** Reviewed 13 peer-reviewed interventions and 10 commercial apps for adults with ADHD in the UK. It is a project report, not a peer-reviewed systematic review — [Strathprints](https://strathprints.strath.ac.uk/89559/).

### Inferences
- Reminders alone are unlikely to sustain use of a planning app. The only adult ADHD RCT shows reminders shift timing (faster return) but not overall engagement. Reminders should open straight onto one small actionable thing that gives immediate value, not a backlog.
- Self-monitoring that relies on frequent prompts loses most users. Keep any check-in to one tap, keep it infrequent, and show the user value from it immediately.
- No commercial ADHD planner or reminder app has RCT evidence of improving task completion in adults. A designer should not claim efficacy.

### Gaps
- No meta-analysis of app-based organisation or reminder tools for adult ADHD with pooled effect sizes was found.
- Kenter 2023 RCT results and Knouse et al. 2022 (usability of a CBT mobile app for adults, PLoS Digit Health) were identified but not read.

## 8. Self-compassion and shame in ADHD; does self-criticism impair follow-through?

### Takeaway
Adults with ADHD report lower self-compassion and more perceived criticism, and low self-compassion is associated with poorer mental health (**Moderate** for the association; cross-sectional, n=1,203). No trial results show that raising self-compassion improves task follow-through in ADHD (**None found**). The link to procrastination runs through self-esteem and emotion regulation (section 5) and is correlational.

### Cited Findings
- **Beaton et al. 2020, University of Sheffield.** 1,203 adults. Those with an ADHD diagnosis had lower self-compassion and higher perceived criticism than those without. People high in ADHD traits but undiagnosed had similar self-compassion to the diagnosed group. Perceived criticism partly explained the gap, even after accounting for mood disorders. Cross-sectional online self-report — [White Rose eprint](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/165039).
- **Beaton doctoral thesis.** Low self-compassion contributes to poorer mental health in adult ADHD, and self-compassion is a potential treatment target — [White Rose eTheses](https://etheses.whiterose.ac.uk/32045).
- **Registered trial NCT04978792.** Tests whether a brief self-guided self-compassion practice raises self-compassion and resilience to criticism in adults with ADHD. No published results found — [ClinicalTrials.gov](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT04978792).
- **Procrastination link.** In college students, self-esteem and emotion dysregulation partly account for the association between ADHD and procrastination — [BSL record](https://mijn.bsl.nl/adhd-symptoms-and-procrastination-in-college-students-the-roles-/23602698).
- **Third-wave components** (mindfulness, acceptance) gave iOR 1.95 for treatment response in the component network meta-analysis. They are adjacent to self-compassion approaches but not the same thing — [PMC11683884](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11683884/).

### Inferences
- Non-judgemental wording carries little risk and is consistent with the evidence. Examples: no red "overdue" walls or streak-loss shaming, framing a missed task as a re-plan rather than a failure, and carrying tasks forward neutrally.
- The claim that this improves follow-through is not established. Present it as harm-avoidance and supporting engagement.

### Gaps
- No experimental or longitudinal evidence was found that self-criticism causally reduces task follow-through in adult ADHD.

## 9. Summary for a designer: which mechanisms are well supported?

### Takeaway
The best-supported mechanisms come from structured organisation and planning in CBT and MCT. One trusted list, a calendar checked daily, prioritisation, breaking tasks into small first steps, and scheduling at specific times are supported at package level (**Strong**) and by component analysis (**Moderate**). If-then cues are next (**Strong** in general populations, **Weak** in ADHD), followed by planning-time scaffolding, based on the prospective-memory deficit profile. Reminders, visual timers, body doubling, gamified rewards and self-compassion framing are plausible and low-risk but **not** demonstrated in adults with ADHD. Reminders tested alone did not improve engagement.

### Cited Findings
- **CBT and MCT packages built around organisation and planning beat attention-matched controls:**
  - Safren 2010: responders 53% vs 23% on CGI — [anhoriga.se](https://anhoriga.se/Artiklar-och-rapporter/2010/cognitive-behavioral-therapy-vs-relaxation-with-educational-support-for-medication-treated-adults-with-adhd-and-persistent-symptoms-a-randomized-controlled-trial)
  - Solanto 2010: responders 53% vs 28% — [Am J Psychiatry](https://psychiatryonline.org/doi/abs/10.1176/appi.ajp.2009.09081123)
  - Knouse 2017: g = 0.65 for symptoms — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28504540/)
- **Organisational strategies as a component:** iOR 2.03 for response — [PMC11683884](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11683884/).
- **Implementation intentions:** d = 0.65 in general populations — [KOPS](https://kops.uni-konstanz.de/entities/publication/2e749bfb-8533-437c-8203-7e788c910c5f). In ADHD only child lab studies exist — [Gawrilow 2011](https://www.socmot.uni-konstanz.de/sites/default/files/Gawrilow_Gollwitzer_Oettingen_Executive_Function_JSCP_2011.pdf).
- **Prospective-memory deficit lies mainly in planning:** d = 1.60, vs d = 0.03 for recall — [PMC3590133](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3590133/).
- **SMS reminders** gave faster logins but no gain in completion — [PMC9149073](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9149073/).

| Mechanism | Evidence in adults with ADHD | Rating |
|---|---|---|
| Single capture list + calendar, reviewed daily; prioritise; schedule into time slots | CBT/MCT RCTs + component NMA | Strong (package) / Moderate (component) |
| Break tasks into a small concrete first step | Part of CBT/MCT packages; problem-solving component | Moderate (as package part) |
| If-then (when/where) cue per task | General population meta-analysis; ADHD child lab studies | Strong general / Weak ADHD |
| Scaffold planning (when, where, first step) more than recall | PM study, n=45+45 | Moderate (deficit), inference for design |
| Push reminders / nudges to open app | One adult RCT: faster return, no completion gain | Moderate (limited benefit) |
| Visual timers / visible time | Deficit meta-analysis; no adult intervention trial | Weak / None found for intervention |
| Immediate rewards / instant progress feedback | Delay discounting meta-analyses (mixed ages) | Weak (theory-consistent) |
| Body doubling / co-working | Surveys, small HCI studies, one null poster | None found / Weak |
| Self-compassionate, non-shaming tone | Cross-sectional associations; trial unpublished | Weak (harm-avoidance) |
| Frequent self-monitoring prompts | Poor engagement (22% replied to all SMS) | Weak, with an engagement risk |

### Inferences
- Engagement is the bottleneck for any self-guided tool, and reminders by themselves do not solve it. Nudges, a low-friction interface and immediate value should be designed together, and the app should be framed as a support for a planning habit, not as a treatment.

### Gaps
- No study was found that tests any of these mechanisms in a single-user, self-guided planner for adults with ADHD. N-of-1 or self-experiment designs would be the realistic way for one user to evaluate them.
